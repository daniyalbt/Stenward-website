Where ISO 27001 stops and AI governance begins

AI Governance 6 November 2026 6 min read

Getting certified to ISO 27001 is a significant piece of work, and once the certificate is in hand it’s natural to expect it to carry most security conversations with customers. For a long time, it did.

Lately, though, security reviews have started to include a section that wasn’t there a year or two ago. It asks whether your product uses AI. Which models. Whether the customer’s data is used for training. What happens to that data once it reaches a third-party provider. Whether a person reviews what the model produces. And somewhere in it, a question the certificate simply doesn’t speak to: how do you govern all of this?

It’s a moment a lot of well-run companies are meeting for the first time, and the certificate they expected to rely on wasn’t designed to answer it.

Why ISO 27001 doesn’t cover your AI

ISO 27001 is a standard for managing information security, and it does that job well. It asks you to identify your risks, decide how to treat them, and show that you operate the controls you claim to. Its Annex A covers access, supplier relationships, change management, incident response, and the other security fundamentals.

What it doesn’t do is address artificial intelligence specifically. It treats an AI system much as it treats any other asset or supplier. That isn’t a shortcoming - it’s simply the scope the standard was written for. The questions a buyer now asks about AI, such as where your training data comes from, whether a model’s output can be explained, how you catch it when it gets something wrong, and what the system is and isn’t meant to be used for, sit outside that scope.

The distinction buyers have noticed

The certificate tells a customer you manage information security. It doesn’t tell them whether you manage AI responsibly. Those have become two different questions.

What buyers are actually asking

The AI section of a security review tends to return to the same concerns, phrased in various ways. None of it is unreasonable. These are the AI versions of questions buyers have asked about data and security for years. The challenge is that an existing evidence pack mostly doesn’t answer them.

Do you use AI in the product or behind the scenes, including third-party providers such as OpenAI or Anthropic?
Does the customer’s data leave your environment to be processed, and is it used to train anyone’s models?
Who are your AI sub-processors, and what do your contracts with them say?
Do you have an acceptable-use policy for staff using AI tools?
Does a person review AI-generated output before it affects the customer, and what happens when the model is wrong?
Do you hold ISO 42001 or follow a recognised AI governance framework?

Where your ISMS already reaches: and where it stops

This is a point that often gets lost. If you already run an ISO 27001 ISMS, you are not starting from zero, and a good deal of the groundwork carries over. A company with a working ISMS can answer a fair share of the AI questionnaire today, by pointing controls it already has at a new kind of asset.

Layer 1 · Your ISMS already covers
Supplier management, including AI providers Data classification and where data may go Access controls across your stack Change management Incident process Risk assessment method
Layer 2 · The AI-specific gap
Assessing the impact of an AI system on the people it affects
Governing the data a model is trained or fine-tuned on
Documenting a model’s intended use and its limits
Showing that a human stays meaningfully involved

That second layer is the genuine gap, and it’s where ISO 42001 comes in.

Where ISO 42001 fits

ISO 42001, published in 2023, is the management-system standard for artificial intelligence. It sits alongside ISO 27001 and works in a familiar way - governance, risk, defined controls, evidence - but focused on the AI lifecycle rather than information security in general. It covers much of what the questionnaire now asks about: AI risk and impact assessment, data governance for AI, human oversight, and accountability for how a system behaves.

For a company already certified to ISO 27001, taking it on is less daunting than it first appears, because the management-system machinery is already running. You would be extending something that works rather than standing up a second system from scratch.

Be realistic about timing

Not every company needs an ISO 42001 certificate today, and the regulatory picture, including the EU AI Act, which we looked at in a separate piece, is still settling. For many SaaS companies the sensible step right now is to close the clear gaps and be able to answer the questionnaire credibly, with a route to formal certification when the risk, or a customer, genuinely calls for it.

What to do about it now

Roughly in order of effort:

Step 1

Begin with an AI inventory. You can’t govern what you haven’t listed, and most companies have more AI in their product and operations than they expect.

Step 2

Assess your AI providers as the suppliers they are, and check what your contracts say about training and data use.

Step 3

Put a short, workable acceptable-use policy in place for staff.

Step 4

Map where customer data actually flows when an AI feature touches it.

Step 5

Map the questionnaire itself against what your ISMS already answers, so you know where the real gaps are rather than guessing.

That much will usually move you from “we can’t answer this” to a considered, evidenced response, which is often what the deal actually needs.
