How Long Does ISO 27001 Certification Actually Take?

ISO 27001 16 October 2026 6 min read

How long does ISO 27001 certification actually take? If a customer has just made it a condition of a deal, that’s the first question on your mind - and it deserves an honest answer, not a marketing one.

6-9
Months
Realistic timeline for most SaaS companies starting from scratch

The Honest Answer

It’s rarely a matter of weeks, and seldom as quick as three months. Companies that already have solid security practices in place tend to move faster, while those with a broad scope, limited internal capacity, or very little to build on will usually need longer. For most, six to nine months is the realistic middle ground, and it’s the figure we’d encourage you to plan around.

There’s also one part of the timeline that no amount of budget or effort can shorten - and it’s worth understanding why early on.

The Two Phases You Need to Understand

ISO 27001 certification isn’t one thing. It’s two sequential phases, and they move at completely different speeds.

1

Build the System

Scope definition. Risk assessment. Policies. Controls. Evidence. How long this takes entirely depends on where you’re starting from.

2

The Certification Audit

Independent body assessment. Governed by fixed rules, so more predictable. But includes something non-negotiable that you cannot compress.

Critical Detail

A company with mature security practices already in place moves through the build phase faster than one starting from scratch. Your existing documentation, controls, and evidence accumulation matter enormously. The gap between these two starting points can easily be several months on its own.

The Part You Cannot Compress

ISO 27001 doesn’t certify that you’ve written a security management system. It certifies that you genuinely operate one. The standard requires evidence that your controls are working in practice, and that you’ve completed an internal audit and a management review before certification. In practice, that means your system needs to have been running for a period before the Stage 2 audit - certification bodies typically look for a few months of operating history, often around three, so there’s enough evidence to show the controls actually function over time rather than on paper.

Shortcuts

This is the part that tends to surprise people. You can bring in an excellent consultant, work evenings and weekends, and have every policy written within a fortnight - but you still can’t be certified until your controls have been running long enough to leave a credible trail. Access reviews have to have actually taken place. Logs have to span real time, not a single afternoon. It’s a floor that effort alone can’t lower.

Myth to Ignore

Headlines promising certification in a matter of weeks almost always describe companies that were already operating mature security controls before they began - so they walked in with the operating history already behind them. If you’re starting with little in place, that operating period is simply part of the journey, and it’s not one you can leap over.

The Actual Timeline

Weeks 1-4

Scoping. Define what gets certified. Narrow scope = faster. Too wide = months of extra work.

Weeks 4-12

Risk assessment. The demanding phase. Most teams underestimate it. It drives everything downstream.

Weeks 8-20

Implementation. Put controls in place and run them. The 3-month waiting period lives here.

Weeks 16-20

Internal audit. You audit your own system first. Non-negotiable. Stage 1 failures trace back to skipping this.

Months 5-8

Certification audits. Stage 1 confirms readiness. Stage 2 tests whether controls actually work. Clear both = certified.

What Actually Derails Timelines

Mistake 1

Scope creep. Certifying the entire company when a narrower scope would have met the customer’s requirement. Kills timelines instantly.

Mistake 2

Weak ownership. Handed to someone without authority to make decisions or free up resources. The project stalls.

Mistake 3

Underestimating documentation. Teams budget weeks. Risk assessment and Statement of Applicability take months.

Mistake 4

Late gap discovery. Problems found at internal audit or Stage 1 force rework. Should surface at scoping.

Mistake 5

Auditor delays. Good certification bodies are booked out months in advance. Plan Stage 1 and 2 early.

The Platform Question

GRC platforms (Vanta, Drata, Sprinto) do one thing really well: continuous evidence collection and monitoring. They pull logs, flag misconfigurations, track policy acknowledgements. That saves real time.

What they aren’t, though, is a management system. A platform can’t decide what scope makes sense for your business, it can’t run your risk assessment, and it can’t justify to an auditor why a particular control doesn’t apply to you. Those are judgement calls, not data-collection tasks.

We’ve seen how this plays out more than once. A company buys a platform, works through the checklist, watches the dashboard turn green, and arrives at the Stage 2 audit feeling confident - only to face questions the tool was never able to answer. The risk assessment turns out to be thin, the scope doesn’t quite match how the business really works, and the Statement of Applicability doesn’t line up with the risks. None of these are things software gets wrong; they’re things software simply can’t do, because they call for judgement rather than monitoring.

What Actually Works

Platform + experienced practitioner. The platform handles evidence. The person handles decisions. Together, it’s the fastest and most reliable route. Platform alone produces a green dashboard and a difficult audit.

Bottom Line

Plan for 6-9 months. If you have SOC 2 or mature security practices, you might hit the faster end. If you’re starting from scratch, protect the 3-month operating period and don’t let anyone tell you it can be skipped.

And treat any timeline that sounds too good to be true with a little caution. The one part of this process nobody can shorten is the part that proves your system genuinely works - which, when you think about it, is the whole point of the certificate.
