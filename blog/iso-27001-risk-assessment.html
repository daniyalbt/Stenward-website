The ISO 27001 Risk Assessment: Where Projects Get Decided

ISO 27001 24 October 2026 7 min read

Ask anyone who has been through ISO 27001 which phase caused the most trouble, and the answer is rarely the audit itself. It's the risk assessment. It looks like a spreadsheet exercise, and that is exactly why it gets underestimated. Everything the rest of the system depends on flows from it, so a weak assessment shows up later as weak controls, a thin Statement of Applicability, and an auditor who asks questions nobody can answer.

Core Outputs
Risk assessment, risk treatment plan, and Statement of Applicability

Why It Matters More Than It Looks

The standard doesn't ask for a list of threats. It asks for a repeatable method, applied consistently, that produces decisions an owner has accepted. Each control in Annex A should be traceable back to a risk you identified, or to a justified reason it doesn't apply. If that chain is broken, the certification audit tends to find it.

What the Standard Actually Asks For

Clause 6.1.2 sets the requirements for the assessment itself. You need to define risk criteria, meaning how you score likelihood and impact and what level of risk you are willing to accept. You need to identify risks to confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information within scope. You need to analyse and evaluate those risks, and you need to assign a risk owner to each one.

Clause 6.1.3 covers treatment. For each risk you decide whether to modify it, retain it, avoid it, or share it. Those decisions feed into the Statement of Applicability, which lists every Annex A control, whether it applies, and why. The SoA is not a formality. It is the document an auditor uses to test whether your assessment actually drove your controls.

Critical Detail

The method has to be consistent. Two people assessing the same risk should land on broadly the same score. If the scores depend on who happened to be in the room, the auditor will treat the whole register with suspicion.

Start With Scope, Not Threats

Most teams jump straight to listing threats. That's backwards. The assessment only covers assets inside your certification scope, so the first job is an asset inventory: the systems, data stores, suppliers, people, and locations that the scope actually includes.

The scope decision and the risk assessment are closely linked. A narrow, well-justified scope means fewer assets to assess and fewer controls to evidence. A scope drawn too wide, usually to satisfy a single customer's wording, multiplies the work without adding much assurance. Settle the scope before the risk register grows, because changing it later means reworking the assessment.

Running It Without Losing Months

Step 1

Agree the risk criteria once. A simple five-point scale for likelihood and impact, plus a clear line for what risk level requires treatment, is enough for most companies.

Step 2

Build the asset register from the scope. Name an owner for each asset. Ownership is what makes the later decisions stick.

Step 3

Identify threats and vulnerabilities per asset, then score each risk. Work through the register in sessions with the people who run the systems, not alone in a document.

Step 4

Decide treatment for each risk and record it in the risk treatment plan. Get the risk owner to accept any risk you retain, in writing.

Step 5

Draft the Statement of Applicability from the treatment decisions. Every control marked as applicable should point to a risk, and every exclusion should have a reason.

Where Teams Get It Wrong

Mistake 1

Generic templates. A downloaded risk list that nobody in the business has reviewed. Auditors recognise it immediately, and it rarely reflects the company's real exposure.

Mistake 2

Scoring without criteria. Every risk rated "high" or "medium" with no definition behind the labels. The results can't be reproduced, so they can't be defended.

Mistake 3

No real risk owners. Risks assigned to a shared mailbox or a role nobody holds. When a risk is accepted, the person accepting it has to have the authority to do so.

Mistake 4

A disconnected SoA. Controls marked as applicable with no link to any risk, or exclusions with vague justifications. This is one of the most common reasons for findings at Stage 2.

Mistake 5

A one-off exercise. The assessment is written once, then left to go stale. Risks change when systems, suppliers, and people change, so the register needs a scheduled review and an update trigger.

Defending It to an Auditor

An auditor doesn't expect a perfect register. They expect a method you can explain, applied the same way throughout, with a visible trail from risk to decision to control. Be ready to walk them through a single risk end to end: how it was scored, who owns it, what treatment was chosen, which controls implement that treatment, and what evidence shows those controls are running.

That trail is also what keeps the assessment alive after certification. Surveillance audits will revisit it, and a register that has been updated through real change is far easier to defend than one that was rewritten the week before the audit.

What Actually Works

A short, explicit method, a register built from real scope, named owners who accept their risks, and an SoA that reads as a summary of those decisions. Teams that get this right spend less time on the audit and more time running the controls.

Bottom Line

Treat the risk assessment as the foundation of the whole management system, not a box to tick on the way to the controls. Settle scope first, agree the criteria before scoring anything, put real owners on every risk, and make sure the Statement of Applicability follows from the decisions you have made.

Get this right and the rest of the project becomes far more predictable. Get it wrong and the problems surface at the worst possible time, usually during the certification audit.
